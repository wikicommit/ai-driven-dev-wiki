"Semantic Diffusion" is the entry on [[Person/martin-fowler]]'s site, dated 14 December 2006, in which he coins [[DefinedTerm/semantic-diffusion]] as a name for the way a coined term's definition is weakened as the term spreads beyond the people who defined it. Fowler opens by describing his own habit of creating neologisms for things he sees in software development, notes that software development "still lacks much useful jargon", and offers semantic diffusion as another candidate addition to it.

The post is framed around two terms he says were undergoing the process publicly as he wrote: "agile" and "Web 2.0", both coined within the preceding few years and both accompanied by lengthy definitions — the agile manifesto together with books and articles by its authors in the first case, and a definitional article by Tim O'Reilly in the second. He reports meeting people who believed agile methods meant doing no planning, and people who believed Web 2.0 was solely about using AJAX.

## Key Points

- Semantic diffusion works like the telephone game: a group other than the originators starts talking about a term without following its original definition, a further group adds its own distortions, and key meaning is lost after a few hand-offs.
- It coincides with an idea's hype phase, and — ironically, in Fowler's framing — strikes popular terms hardest, since unpopular terms have fewer people to form the telephone chain.
- Desirable-sounding names are more exposed. Fowler credits Kent Beck with deliberately picking "Extreme Programming" because "extreme" is often used as a pejorative, but argues a less desirable name reduces rather than avoids the problem, citing the neutral term "object-oriented" as having suffered anyway.
- Broad concepts diffuse more readily than concrete technologies; Extreme Programming's list of concrete practices may be less exposed than agile's broader values and principles.
- Fowler is "more sanguine" than despairing, having watched "object-oriented" and "patterns" recover their meaning years later, and says he prefers the hype to ignorance.
- He rejects abandoning a diffused term for a new one, on the grounds that a new term adds confusion and only repeats the process, and prefers to keep re-articulating the terminology while pointing to those who understand its original meaning.
- He notes a further complication: terms genuinely shift over time, so originators also have a responsibility to say how the ideas evolve and to point at the new people driving that evolution — with a "tricky balance between holding to a clear definition and dogmatism".

## Context

The entry carries a 2006 date but also contains a "Semantic Inversion" section crediting Holly Cummins with coining that term for the narrower case in which a term ends up meaning the opposite of what it was coined to describe — Fowler's examples being "DevOps" becoming the name for a separated operations team and "Minimal Viable Product" being used for a $12M first release.

Nearly two decades after publication the post re-entered current practice discourse: on 23 March 2025 [[Person/simon-willison]] cited it while complaining that the definition of [[DefinedTerm/vibe-coding]] was being distorted, quoted Fowler's definition as "very clear", and called the vibe coding case "such a clear example of this effect in action".
