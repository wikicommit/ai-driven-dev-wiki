"Model Context Protocol (MCP) Security" is a report produced by Workstream 4 of the Coalition for Secure AI — Secure Design Patterns for Agentic Systems — and approved by the CoSAI Project Governing Board on 8 January 2026. It is marked **Status: Draft**. Its subject is the security of [[DefinedTerm/model-context-protocol]] implementations rather than of the specification in the abstract: it analyses the June 2025 (`2025-06-18`) and later revised specification's transport and protocol layers, threat-modelling strategies for MCP-based agentic systems, supply chain considerations for servers and tools, identity and access management in agentic architectures, deployment and operation practices, and recommendations for protocol enhancements. The report states that its authors are collaborating with [[Organization/anthropic]] and the MCP maintainer community to keep the recommendations implementable, and that the work coordinates with CoSAI's Software Supply Chain Security workstream.

Its scope exclusions are stated as explicitly as its scope: it does not address content safety, misinformation or AI ethics unrelated to security; general model safety or alignment beyond security implications; detailed implementation of specific security tools, which it defers to follow-on papers; non-security aspects of MCP performance, scalability or functional capability; and legal or regulatory compliance, which it routes to CoSAI's Workstream 3 on AI Risk Governance. The primary audience is security professionals and developers securing, creating or connecting to MCP servers, with MCP maintainers and core protocol developers named as a longer-term audience.

## Key Findings

- **Nearly forty threats across twelve categories.** MCP-T1 improper authentication and identity management; T2 missing or improper access control; T3 input validation and sanitization failures; T4 input/instruction — elsewhere data/control — boundary distinction failure; T5 inadequate data protection and confidentiality controls; T6 missing integrity and verification controls; T7 session and transport security failures; T8 network binding and isolation failures; T9 trust boundary and privilege design failures; T10 absent resource management and rate limiting; T11 supply chain and lifecycle security failures; T12 insufficient logging, monitoring and auditability.
- **Three specificity tiers.** Tier 1 covers seven threats novel to MCP's architecture and design decisions; Tier 2 covers eight known threats that manifest differently or are amplified in MCP contexts; Tier 3 covers nineteen conventional threats broadly applicable or deriving from legacy, infrastructure or transport decisions.
- **The Tier 1 threats named include** identity spoofing, [[DefinedTerm/tool-poisoning]], full schema poisoning, resource content poisoning, typosquatting and confusion attacks, shadow MCP servers, and overreliance on the LLM. The report distinguishes carefully between the poisoning variants: full schema poisoning compromises the entire structural definition and type system of tools rather than individual metadata, while resource content poisoning hides instructions in the backend data sources a server retrieves rather than in tool definitions at all.
- **MCP needs its own threat model.** Existing frameworks such as MITRE ATLAS, the NIST AI RMF and MAESTRO are acknowledged as high quality, but the report argues they "assume components behave predictably according to predefined logic", whereas MCP "places an LLM, an agent whose behavior is shaped by natural language input, at the center of security-critical decisions." The four considerations it names as new are protocol-level authentication between clients and tool servers, dynamic capability negotiation determining what tools an AI can reach, distributed trust across independent tool providers, and session management complexities specific to long-lived AI conversations.
- **Deployment pattern determines the threat model.** Three patterns are set out — all-local, single-tenant hybrid, and multi-tenant cloud — and the report argues security posture depends on where server code originates (first-party, open source, third-party), where it executes, and what it can access. Local stdio deployments give process-level isolation but need filesystem access managed; internal Streamable HTTP deployments must consider lateral movement and authentication; external cloud deployments introduce internet-facing surface and multi-tenancy concerns.
- **Eleven control families** are mapped against the threat categories: agent identity, secure delegation and access control, input and data sanitization and filtering, cryptographic integrity and remote attestation, sandboxing and isolation, cryptographic verification of resources, transport layer security, secure tool and UX design, human-in-the-loop, logging, and lifecycle and governance.
- **Consent fatigue undercuts the specification's own defence.** The report notes MCP provides guidance on making tool origins and inputs visible and recommends human-in-the-loop approval, but that users reflexively approving prompts without careful review "can significantly undermine these protections" — and it lists insecure human-in-the-loop and consent/user approval fatigue as threats in their own right.

## Basis & Caveats

The evidence base is documented incidents plus a constructed taxonomy rather than original measurement. The report states that multiple critical CVEs have been reported and that incidents such as data leakage have already occurred across MCP and agentic deployments, and names three: an Asana AI incident of May 2025 in which a tenant isolation flaw allowed cross-organization data contamination affecting up to 1,000 enterprises; a WordPress plugin vulnerability affecting over 100,000 sites through privilege escalation via MCP in the AI Engine plugin, patched 18 June 2025; and a Supabase MCP issue in which researchers demonstrated prompt injection via support ticket data causing AI tools to expose private tables through a connected server with direct database access, exploiting excessive tools and overprivilege. Its own summary of these is that they show the threats "are active threats, not theoretical concerns."

Two limits are worth carrying with the findings. The document is marked "Status: Draft" and carries the opaque version string `7ec1306f2f55563f6eeef9d36a6bb2b531491ceb` rather than a numbered release. And CoSAI's contributor list is drawn largely from vendors with commercial stakes in the area — the workstream leads, editors and contributors named include people from IBM, Intel, NVIDIA, Anthropic, Google, Cisco, Red Hat, Wiz, Amazon, Dell, PayPal, ServiceNow, Trend Micro and Adversa AI — which the report itself frames positively as an open ecosystem of AI and security experts from industry-leading organizations. The report also discloses a policy on AI-assisted drafting, mandating human-reviewed or -edited final outputs and disclosure of substantial AI use.

The recommended posture in its conclusion is defence in depth: zero-trust architectures, hardware-based isolation through trusted execution environments, rigorous supply chain vetting, and continuous monitoring — with the observation that securing MCP requires coordinated effort across developers, organizations and protocol maintainers.
