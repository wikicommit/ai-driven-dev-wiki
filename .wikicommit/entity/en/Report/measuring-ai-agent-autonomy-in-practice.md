[[Organization/anthropic]] published this study on 18 February 2026 as a first empirical look at how
people actually deploy and use AI agents. It analyzed millions of human-agent interactions using the
company's privacy-preserving analysis tooling, asking how much autonomy people grant agents, how that
changes as they gain experience, which domains agents operate in, and whether the actions agents take
are risky.

For the study, an agent is defined as an AI system equipped with tools that allow it to take actions,
such as running code, calling external APIs or sending messages to other agents. The study draws on
two data sources with opposite trade-offs. On the public API it analyzes individual tool calls across
thousands of customers, which gives breadth but cannot reconstruct how actions compose into longer
sessions. In [[SoftwareApplication/claude-code]], Anthropic's own coding agent, it can link requests
into whole sessions, which makes it the main source for measuring autonomy, but it covers only one
product. Much of the classification — of risk, autonomy, task complexity, human involvement and the
reasons for stopping — was itself done by Claude, validated against internal data where possible.

## Findings

- **Claude Code works autonomously for longer.** Most turns are short: the median lasts around 45
  seconds and has stayed between 40 and 55 seconds. At the tail, the 99.9th-percentile turn duration
  nearly doubled between October 2025 and January 2026, from under 25 minutes to over 45 minutes. The
  increase was smooth across model releases, which the authors read as a sign that it is not purely a
  result of increased capability. Among Anthropic's internal users, the success rate on the most
  challenging tasks doubled from August to December while average human interventions per session fell
  from 5.4 to 3.3. The authors take both measurements as pointing to a
  [[DefinedTerm/deployment-overhang]]: the autonomy models can handle exceeds what they exercise in
  practice.
- **Experienced users auto-approve more, but also interrupt more.** Newer users (fewer than 50
  sessions) use full auto-approve in roughly 20% of sessions, rising to over 40% by 750 sessions.
  Interrupt rates rise too, from about 5% of turns for users with around 10 sessions to around 9% for
  more experienced users. The study interprets this as a shift in oversight strategy from approving
  each action to letting the agent run and stepping in when something goes wrong. On the public API,
  87% of tool calls on minimal-complexity tasks involve some human, against 67% on high-complexity
  tasks.
- **Claude Code pauses for clarification more often than humans interrupt it.** On the most complex
  tasks it asks for clarification more than twice as often as on minimal-complexity tasks, and more
  often than humans interrupt it. The most common reasons it stops itself are to present a choice
  between approaches (35%) and to gather diagnostic information or test results (21%); the most common
  reason humans interrupt is to provide missing technical context or corrections (32%). The authors
  caution that Claude may not be stopping at the right moments and that product features such as plan
  mode may affect the behaviour.
- **Agents are used in risky domains, but not yet at scale.** In a sample of 998,481 public-API tool
  calls, 80% came from agents that appear to have at least one safeguard, 73% appear to have a human in
  the loop in some way, and only 0.8% of actions appear irreversible. Software engineering accounts for
  nearly 50% of tool calls, with smaller use in areas such as business intelligence, customer service,
  sales, finance and e-commerce. The riskiest clusters involved security-related actions, financial
  transactions and medical information, and the authors expect many of them to be evaluations rather
  than production use.

The study lists its own limitations: it covers a single model provider; API traffic is analyzed as
isolated tool calls; the classifications are generated by Claude; it reflects late 2025 to early 2026
only; and tool-call sampling over-represents deployments that make many sequential calls.

Its recommendations are that model and product developers invest in post-deployment monitoring, that
model developers train models to recognize their own uncertainty, and that product developers design
for user oversight. It argues that it is too early to mandate specific interaction patterns such as
requiring humans to approve every action, and concludes that the autonomy agents exercise in practice
is co-constructed by the model, the user and the product. See also
[[DefinedTerm/human-in-the-loop]].
