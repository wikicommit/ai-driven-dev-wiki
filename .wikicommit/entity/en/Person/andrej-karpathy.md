Andrej Karpathy coined the term [[DefinedTerm/vibe-coding]] on 6 February 2025, in a tweet describing "a new kind of coding" in which, as he put it, you "fully give in to the vibes, embrace exponentials, and forget that the code even exists". He attributed its feasibility to LLMs getting "too good", naming Cursor Composer with Sonnet as an example, and said he talked to the tool via SuperWhisper rather than touching the keyboard much.

Karpathy qualified the approach in the same tweet, calling it "not too bad for throwaway weekend projects, but still quite amusing" — a qualification [[Person/simon-willison]] later argued the authors and publishers of two forthcoming books had missed by not reading to the end of the tweet.

## Background

Karpathy is a computer scientist, a co-founder of OpenAI, and a former AI leader at Tesla. His writing on how to work with language models predates the term he is best known for: the concept of vibe coding elaborates on a claim he made in 2023 that "the hottest new programming language is English," meaning that LLM capabilities were such that humans would no longer need to learn specific programming languages to command computers.

He has also supplied two of the framings that organise current practitioner writing on [[DefinedTerm/context-engineering]]. LangChain's [[BlogPosting/context-engineering]] attributes to him the analogy that LLMs are like a new kind of operating system, with the model as the CPU and the context window as RAM serving as working memory, and quotes his description of context engineering as the "delicate art and science of filling the context window with just the right information for the next step."

The tweet that coined vibe coding describes Karpathy's own working practice at the time: asking for "the dumbest things" he was too lazy to do himself, always choosing "Accept All" and no longer reading the diffs, pasting error messages back in with no comment, and working around bugs the model could not fix by asking for random changes until they went away. He characterised the result as "not really coding — I just see stuff, say stuff, run stuff, and copy paste stuff, and it mostly works".

In March 2025 Karpathy replied directly to [[Person/simon-willison]]'s [[BlogPosting/not-all-ai-assisted-programming-is-vibe-coding]] with a qualification of his own coinage. He wrote that "it will take some time to settle on definitions", and that while he personally uses the term when he feels like the dog in the "I have no idea what I'm doing" GIF — citing an iOS app he had built the night before as an example — "in practice I rarely go full out vibe coding, and more often I still look at the code, I add complexity slowly and I try to learn over time how the pieces work, to ask clarifying questions etc."

## Works & Achievements

That February 2025 tweet is the origin of the term vibe coding. Willison notes that within 84 days of the tweet, two forthcoming books were using the term in a sense contrary to Karpathy's description, which he attributes to three authors and the staff of two publishers not reading to the end of the tweet.

The term itself spread far beyond its origin: Merriam-Webster listed it as a "slang & trending" expression in March 2025, and it was named *Collins English Dictionary* Word of the Year for 2025. Not everyone in the field welcomed it — in June 2025 Andrew Ng took issue with the name on the grounds that it misleads people into assuming software engineers just "go with the vibes" when using AI tools.
