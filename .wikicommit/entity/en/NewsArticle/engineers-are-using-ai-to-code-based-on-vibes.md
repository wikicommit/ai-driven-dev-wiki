"Engineers Are Using AI to Code Based on Vibes" is an 8 April 2025 IEEE Spectrum news article by contributing editor Matthew S. Smith, which appeared in the June 2025 print issue as "Engineers Are Using AI for 'Vibe Coding.'" It reports on how practitioners were actually using the technique two months after [[Person/andrej-karpathy]] named it, and opens with the author's own reaction to Karpathy's viral post — "Wait! Am I a vibe coder?" — having already used LLMs to revamp his personal website and build several small personal projects despite limited programming knowledge.

The article's structure is three practitioner accounts followed by a disagreement about whether any of them count. Its reporting value is in the concrete before-and-after figures the interviewees give, and in surfacing a definitional dispute that would recur throughout 2025.

## Key Points

- **Karpathy's definition as the article states it.** On 2 February, Karpathy — described as a computer scientist and founding member of OpenAI — defined vibe coding as a style of programming where software engineers "give in to the vibes, embrace exponentials, and forget that the code even exists."
- **A mechanical engineer shipping internal tools.** Prasad Naik, a licensed professional mechanical engineer at Gripple, converted an iPad app he had originally written in C over the course of almost a month into a modern JavaScript web app in about two hours using step-by-step directions from ChatGPT, estimating that more than 90 percent of the code he used was generated. He then built a more complex server-connected app in about a week and a half, and says he doubts he could have done it without AI tools.
- **Proof of concepts as the use case.** Jason Touleyrou, a data engineering lead at Corewell Health, uses AI-assisted coding to test ideas quickly — "speed to ideation is critical" — and built a coffee tracking tool using Google Cloud services he had never touched professionally. His stated key is workflow: start from a well-defined idea, then have the AI generate code, then move it into an advisory role for debugging, while continuing to use quality tools such as linters.
- **The dissent.** Taher Vohra, a software engineer with 25 years of experience, argues Naik and Touleyrou are not vibe coding at all. Taking the definition literally — "that I will not look at code" — he found that "specifying what I want the AI to do is turning out to be a harder problem than doing it myself." He is not against AI for programming and uses multiple assistants including Cursor, but stresses that developers writing production-ready code must keep their eyes on the code.
- **The point they agree on.** All three interviewees agree vibe coding helps engineers gain skills in languages and stacks they do not yet know. Vohra's advice to junior engineers is to treat it as an accelerator for gaining new skills rather than a speed improvement on skills they already have — his example being a front-end engineer becoming full-stack "in a few months, as opposed to two years."

## Context

The article is an early piece of reporting on a term that was about two months old at publication, and its framing question is whether careful, reviewed AI-assisted work counts as vibe coding at all. It is a single-outlet account built on three interviews, and the productivity figures in it are the interviewees' own estimates rather than measured results.
