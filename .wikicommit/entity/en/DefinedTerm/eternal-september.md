Eternal September originally names a moment in Usenet's history: each September a wave of new
university students arrived online unfamiliar with its norms and was patiently onboarded, until
mainstream dial-up ISPs brought a continuous influx of new users and it became "the September that
never ended". In a February 2026 post
([[BlogPosting/welcome-to-the-eternal-september-of-open-source]]), GitHub applies the term to open
source, arguing that it is now experiencing its own Eternal September — driven not just by new users
but by the sheer volume of contributions, as generative AI lets people produce code, issues and
security reports at scale while the cost of reviewing them stays the same.

## Usage

In GitHub's usage the term frames a trust problem rather than a quality problem alone. Open
collaboration runs on trust, which the friction of contributing used to protect; the post argues that
most contributors act in good faith, but that when volume accelerates faster than review capacity
even well-intentioned submissions can overwhelm maintainers, and trust starts to strain. GitHub treats
it as a continuation of older problems — noisy inbound, triage of bug reports, automated scanner
reports — at a new scale.

The post uses the metaphor optimistically as well: it calls open source's Eternal September a sign that
more people want to participate than ever, and argues that, as the early internet evolved norms and
tools to sustain community at scale, open source needs better signals and tools for maintainers rather
than raised drawbridges. Responses it describes range from platform controls on who may open pull
requests to community trust systems such as [[SoftwareApplication/vouch]] and written
[[DefinedTerm/ai-contribution-policy]] rules.
